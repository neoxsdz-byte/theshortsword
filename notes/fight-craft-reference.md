# Fight craft reference

Named fights worth studying, from the works recommended 10 Oct 2026, with what each one solves. Built for the choreography problem: keeping combat legible once power levels pass the point where a reader can measure anything.

Ordered by how directly useful each work is to *The Shortsword*.

**Accuracy note:** chapter and volume numbers below are from memory and are approximate. The fight names and what happens in them are reliable; the numbers are a starting point for finding them, not a citation. Verify before quoting any of it.

---

## Hunter × Hunter (Togashi)

The most useful study on this list. Nen's rules are published to the reader and never change; fights are won by exploiting constraints, not by out-powering.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Kurapika vs Uvogin** | Yorknew City arc, ~ch 85–88 | The definitive weaker-fighter-beats-a-monster fight. Kurapika's power comes from a self-imposed vow with a death penalty attached. Power bought with restriction. |
| **Gon vs Hisoka** | Heaven's Arena, ~ch 60s | A fight that is actually a lesson being administered. The loser gains more than the winner. |
| **Team vs Razor** | Greed Island, ~ch 160s | Rules-as-arena: a dodgeball game with lethal stakes. Teaches that an arbitrary constraint makes a fight *more* tense, not less. |
| **Zeno & Netero vs Meruem** | Chimera Ant, ~ch 255 | Three-way simultaneous assault, perfectly timed, against someone stronger than all three. Staging under a clock. |
| **Netero vs Meruem** | Chimera Ant, ~ch 300–305 | Humanity's peak against something better than humanity. The 100-Type, then the Poor Man's Rose. **A fight deliberately lost in order to win.** |
| **Gon vs Pitou** | Chimera Ant, ~ch 306–308 | Transformation as horror. The price is paid and never refunded. |
| **Hisoka vs Chrollo** | Heaven's Arena, ch ~351–357 | Preparation, environment weaponisation, and conditions that keep operating after a fighter is dead. |

**Steal:** the narrator stopping the fight to state a technique's conditions. It raises tension instead of killing it, because the reader can now do the arithmetic on who loses.

## Cradle (Will Wight)

Twelve books, from a boy who can't fight to continent-breakers, and it stays readable because of a hard public ranking system. The crudest solution to the measurement problem, and it works.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Lindon vs Jai Long** | *Blackflame* (bk 3) | The first real duel with a stated rank gap. The whole book is the preparation; the fight is the payment. |
| **The Uncrowned King tournament** | *Uncrowned* (bk 7) | Highest fight-density volume in the series. A dozen duels, every one legible, every one a different problem. |
| **Wintersteel finale** | *Wintersteel* (bk 8) | Where the scale breaks open and the ranking system has to stretch. Watch how he keeps it countable. |
| **Dreadgod fights** | *Bloodline*, *Reaper* and around | City-and-continent scale. Study what he shows breaking, and what he refuses to describe. |

**Steal:** the ranking system itself. A one-rank upset thrills, a four-rank upset terrifies, and the reader knows the difference without being told. Your book has nothing like this yet.

## Worm (Wildbow)

The closest structural cousin: street-level to world-killing entities, with a protagonist whose power is bugs. Every fight is problem-solving on a clock.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Taylor vs Lung** | Arc 1, *Gestation* | A weak power applied precisely to the one thing it can reach. Best single opening lesson on the list. |
| **Leviathan** | Arc 8, *Extermination* | **The model for an organised defence against something that outclasses everyone present.** Multiple teams, named casualties, a city coming apart. This is your siege at larger scale. |
| **Echidna** | Arcs ~17–18 | Multi-team coordination against an enemy that turns your own people into the threat. |
| **Behemoth / New Delhi** | *Scourge* arc | The second Endbringer, and the cost accounting is merciless. |
| **Taylor vs Alexandria** | *Cell* arc | A fight that is unwinnable on paper, won entirely on a constraint. Possibly the best pure exploitation victory in English prose. |
| **The Scion finale** | Arcs ~29–30 | The absolute endpoint of escalation. Useful mainly as a warning about what happens to prose at that altitude. |

**Steal:** the Endbringer structure — everyone shows up, everyone has a role, the enemy is not defeated, and the chapter is scored in who died and what ground was lost.

## Berserk (Miura)

Tonally the nearest thing to what you're writing: grounded brutality escalating into Apostles and the Godhand. Same lineage as Vinland Saga, but it goes where your book is going.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Guts vs Bazuso** | Golden Age, ~vol 3 | The establishing duel. Scale set by one man against one man. |
| **Guts vs Griffith (first and second duels)** | ~vols 3–4 and ~vol 9 | **The same fight twice with different outcomes.** The second one means something only because the first exists. |
| **Guts vs Zodd** | ~vol 5 | Meeting something that cannot be beaten, and surviving as the only victory available. |
| **The Hundred Man Slayer** | Golden Age, ~vols 9–10 | Attrition choreography. Exhaustion tracked honestly across a hundred opponents. |
| **The Eclipse** | ~vols 12–13 | Helplessness at scale. Nobody wins anything; it's still the best sequence in the work. |
| **Guts vs Grunbeld** | Millennium Falcon, ~vols 26–27 | Post-escalation Guts against armour that negates him. Power creep handled by changing the problem. |

**Steal:** weight. Wounds persist across chapters, the sword is heavy in every frame, and fatigue is never forgotten for narrative convenience.

## Jujutsu Kaisen (Gege Akutami)

Rule-dense high-power combat. Relevant specifically because power is *purchased* with restriction.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Yuta vs Geto** | *Volume 0* | A complete arc of a fight in one volume. Clean to study. |
| **Yuji & Todo vs Hanami** | Goodwill Event arc | Two fighters synchronising mid-fight. Rare and hard to write. |
| **Gojo vs Jogo** | Shibuya Incident | How to demonstrate an absurd power gap in a way that's still interesting to read. |
| **Sukuna vs Mahoraga** | Shibuya Incident | An opponent that adapts to any technique. Forces a win by category change rather than force. |
| **Maki's rampage** | Perfect Preparation arc | Someone with no cursed energy at all beating sorcerers. Constraint as identity. |
| **Gojo vs Sukuna** | Shinjuku Showdown, ~ch 221–236 | The biggest fight in the work. Study the *binding vows* — power traded for stated restriction, declared out loud mid-combat. |

**Steal:** binding vows. This is the worked example of the one-constraint-that-never-bends problem flagged as S5 in `structural-critique.md`.

## Jade City / Jade War / Jade Legacy (Fonda Lee)

Lower power ceiling, best prose choreography on the list. If the problem is sentence-level — how to write a fast exchange so a reader can see it — read her and nothing else here.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Hilo vs Gont Asch, the clean-blade duel** | *Jade City*, climax | Formal duel with rules, honour and consequence. The best single piece of martial prose in modern fantasy. |
| **The docks ambush** | *Jade City* | Street fight with jade abilities, written so you always know where everyone is standing. |
| **Shae vs Ayt Mada** | *Jade Legacy*, late | A duel that is the resolution of three books of politics. |

**Steal:** her sentence rhythm in close combat. Short clauses, concrete verbs, position restated often enough that the reader never loses the room.

## Vagabond (Inoue)

Almost no power scaling. On this list for the opposite reason to Cradle.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Musashi vs Inshun** | Hōzōin spear arc | Weapon-versus-weapon as a philosophical argument. |
| **Musashi vs Denshichiro** | Yoshioka arc | A duel decided in a single exchange after an enormous build. |
| **Musashi vs the Yoshioka school (70 men)** | Yoshioka arc, the rice field | The most famous sequence in the work. Attrition, terrain, and the mind going somewhere else. |
| **Musashi vs Kojiro** | The final duel | Stillness as the whole fight. |

**Steal:** the moment before contact. Inoue spends more panels on distance, breathing and stance than on the strike, and the strike lands harder for it.

## Reverend Insanity

Xianxia, extremely dark, protagonist wins while outclassed, almost always by scheming. Good antidote to solving fights with escalation. Unfinished, and the protagonist is genuinely monstrous — know what you're picking up.

Rather than individual duels, study the **plan-execution structure**: Fang Yuan enters nearly every confrontation having already arranged the terrain, the information and the opponent's assumptions. The combat is the collection, not the contest.

**Steal:** the habit of winning before the fight starts. Directly applicable to Caen, and to Ryn.

## The Stormlight Archive (Sanderson)

Mostly here for one principle, stated by the author: **a magic system's ability to resolve a conflict satisfyingly is proportional to how well the reader understands it.** That sentence is the whole answer to S5.

| Fight | Where | What it solves |
|---|---|---|
| **Szeth kills Gavilar** | *The Way of Kings*, prologue | Opens the series by showing an unknown system doing impossible things — and it works *because* it's the opening, before any rules are owed. |
| **Adolin's four-on-one duel** | *Words of Radiance* | The best small fight Sanderson has written. Clear rules, clear stakes, no cosmic scale required. |
| **Kaladin vs Szeth** | *Words of Radiance*, climax | Two users of the same system at different levels of understanding. |
| **Battle of Thaylen Field** | *Oathbringer*, climax | Enormous scale resolved by a character decision rather than a power increase. |

## Cross-cutting principles

What every fight above has in common, and what to apply regardless of power level:

1. **Give the reader a unit.** Ranks, technique categories, something countable. Without a measuring stick, levelling a mountain and levelling a city read identically.
2. **Cost is the only tension left when nobody can be hurt.** Time, terrain, bystanders, a technique that can't be used twice, a vow that kills you if you break it.
3. **Show scale through what breaks, never through adjectives.**
4. **Geography matters more at high power, not less.** Oda is obsessive about where everyone is standing, and so is Fonda Lee.
5. **State the rules, then have someone win by using them.** Every fight on this list does this. None of them are won by a number going up.
6. **One clear idea per exchange.** Complexity across the fight, simplicity inside each beat.

## What *The Shortsword* already does right

**Ch 21, the Blighted Murel.** Three rules are stated — it absorbs bullets, it re-fuses when split, it smothers fire with acid — and the win comes from Mara's fire ring, a single tactic that beats all three at once. Varen's strength isn't what ends it.

That is the template, and it was written before this list existed. The risk going up the power curve isn't that the choreography can't be done; it's that when the numbers get large enough it stops *feeling* necessary, and the fight becomes a trade of enormous blows instead of a problem being solved.
